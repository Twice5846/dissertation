\chapter*{Preliminary Philosophical Considerations}

A peculiar intellectual roadblock halted my initial attempt to conduct this research before it began.
The fundamental question I faced concerned the nature of research itself.
How could I proceed to conduct research without first explicating what research precisely is?
Upon inquiry, I came to regard research as the process of attaining the aim of any scientific endeavor:
\textit{expanding the body of human knowledge}.

This definition, however, generated a vexing new question and led me down the rabbit hole of epistemology:
\textit{What is knowledge}?
Linguistically, this question constitutes a sensical inquiry.
To answer it -- or any question -- I would need to \textit{know} the answer, thus employing knowledge to define the concept of knowledge, which is evidently nonsensical.

The reader might possess a peripheral awareness of the problem I faced and perhaps has conquered this obstacle in their own work in some way or another.
I, however, could not proceed without addressing this foundational issue.

I acknowledge, I can -- in the format of this text -- use mere words to communicate my thoughts.
My first request, thus, is for you to conspire with me and accept that what I will attempt to say, can, in fact, be verbalized.
Otherwise, how may the nature of knowledge be examined when the words employed to that end are required to be true and thus contain knowledge themselves?

\textit{Note}: I conciously decided on the term \textit{word}, to differentiate it from \textit{language} and other symbols.
See, also, the \enquote{Triangle of Reference} \parencite{ogden-23}.

% Postulate of Shared Meaning
\gap

To begin, let us face a meta-ontological question and ask how we can define (in words) any concept (including knowledge) that we can grasp with our mind.
Axiomatically, we must accept that our cognition will map the concepts in our mind \textit{appropriately} onto the words we say.
This statement remains a necessary assumption for any well-meaning discussion.
Any attempt to investigate what constitues this appropriateness (i.\,e. circumstances, time, individualities) is futile, because the spoken words are the only gateway to the mind of the speaker \parencite{wittgenstein-53}.
Any attempt to skip the words and move our focus towards our own mind would inevitably lead to methodological solipsism \parencite[136]{putnam-75}, which would render any communication about the concepts contained within the mind redundant.

% Show that evaluating pure truth of linguistic statements is impossible because the definitions of words that are used to express the statements that needs to be evaluated need to be given ad infinitum

Furthermore, evaluating the pure truth of linguistic statements is impossible due to the infinite regress inherent in defining words.
Every word used to express a statement relies on a definition composed of other words, which themselves require further definitions.
This creates an endless chain where each definition depends on another, extending ad infinitum.
As a result, attempting to fully comprehend a statement through its linguistic components leads us into an infinite loop of definitions without ever arriving at a fundamental understanding.
This infinite regress prevents us from establishing the absolute truth of any statement based solely on language, as the foundational meanings necessary for such an evaluation are perpetually out of reach.
This underscores the inherent limitations of language as a medium for conveying pure, unequivocal truth.

% Knowledge for myself is nonsensical.
It follows, then, that words can only obtain \textit{meaning} when they -- not their truth, but rather their sense -- can be validated against something outside the mind of the speaker.
That is, when no one hears me, speaking itself becomes nonsensical \parencite{wittgenstein-33}.
I can call the sky \enquote{blue} today, and \enquote{green} tomorrow and \enquote{red} the day after.
And each statement would be true and would have to pass as knowledge, because there is no way (and no need) to proof it.
Everything I would say under these circumstances becomes true by default.

% Postulate of shared meaning. But how?
Let us, thus, postulate that knowledge can only exist \textit{intersubjectively}.
Which means, that the words I use to verbalize my mind's concepts must correspond -- when listened to -- to \textit{equivalent} concepts in the mind of the listener.
How can we be sure that our words correspond precisely to equivalent concepts in the minds of others?

We cannot, as others' minds are inaccessible to us.
This leads us to consider the role of \textit{communal verification} in the establishment of knowledge.
Statements may become true through social endorsement and collective agreement.

Is this an arbitrary agreementh, though, or is there a deeper, more fundamental basis for shared meaning?

% Postulate of Pragmatism
\gap

% Quick detour over nature and research in natural science
Even with no other human listening to us, we can never speak into the void.
Nature will always be there.
We can not lie to nature nor escape it.
However, nature does not speak any natural language.
Thus, we can not ask it to explain itself.
We may only ever ask: yes or no?
And we do that by executing actions, derived from our believes and expressed in natural language, against nature and investigating the resonance.
Over time we can build theories about nature and express them in words under the assumption that other humans interpreting these words have the same understanding and use the same words to describe the phenomena witnessed in nature \parencite[1011b]{aristotle-33}.
This is the basis for any research in the natural sciences.
Because the static and tangible subject of research allows us to build theories and retreat to the methods of fallibilism.
The natural sciences escape any definition of knowledge by this.
Instead, buidling their argument on top of pragmatism: Truth is a means to an end to make decisions.
Can we use the same methodological approach and the same epistemological paradigms when considering a contingent research subject like information systems and the humand mind \parencite{frank-07}?
See, \enquote{Correspondence Theory of Truth} \parencite{david-22}.

Again, how can we be sure that the words we speak will evoke the same mental concepts in the listener as in ourselves?
To repel any ad absurdum discussions about the \enquote{nature of reality}, let us follow a phenomenological approach to this question \parencite{husserl-02}.
See, \enquote{Twin Earth thought experiment} \parencite{putnam-73}.

One approach is to, again, consider words to be our portal into the human mind (see above) and enter a \textit{dialogue} with other humans about what constitutes knowledge.
But which words -- which knowledge -- will ultimately be accepted?
We will accept that, which is most useful and effective to facilitate successful actions in practical contexts.
This means, that truth -- among humans -- is determined by the practical outcomes of holding certain beliefs.
See, the \enquote{Pragmatic Theory of Truth} \parencite{capps-23}.

This leads me to posulate that knowledge exists sui generis, in a class of its own.
Knowledge is not contingent upon explanation, but on its inherent capacity to produce practical outcomes.
Truth is, what is practical, what is useful, what is effective.
We do not need to explicitly break knowledge down into further components like justification and belief.
See, \enquote{Knowledge and its Limits} \parencite{williamson-02}.

But what is a \enquote{practical outcome}?
How can we evaluate the pragmatism of something when we have no mirror -- like nature -- to hold up against our believes and actions.

% Postulate of the Perspective / the Will
\gap

Evaluating the pragmatism of something necessitates an acknowledgment of different \textit{perspectives}.
Because the individual and its context dictate how word configurations -- potential truths -- are understood and interpreted.
What is useful for someone may not be useful for another.
But how then, could we find universal agreement on the pragmatism of something?

I see 3 possible resolutions.
First, consider the individual to be the source for evaluating pragmatism.
This would lead to a solipsistic approach, which I have already refuted.
Second, consider the collective to be the source for evaluating pragmatism.
This would lead to a democratic approach, which has little to do with the intellectual pursuit of knowledge; too much loss and friction.
Third, consider as pragmatic that which enhances the potential for usefulness.
That, which increases our understanding of the world and offers insights but does not prescribe them.

% Summary
\gap

I wanted to define what knowledge is.
I have first followed a philosophy of language approach and asked how we can define, using words, \textit{anything}.
We have seen that shared meaning is a requirement.

How can we proof that we actually mean the same thing by the words we say?
I have looked at the natural sciences and stated that it is the pragmatic content that makes knowledge knowledge.
This means, knowledge is not something that statements possess but rather that knowledge is the thing that has the most benefit toward and end.
This also means that we can never know anything for certain.
We can just know enough for a specific goal.

To advance these thoughts one step further and answer what pragmatism is and how it can be evaluated, I decided on the expansion of the linguistically accessible universe.

\textbf{The ultimate contribution of this dissertation is to enhance our ability to bring about practical outcomes in information systems by expanding, resolving and properly arranging the universe of discourse}.
